# Meera's voice — patterns extracted from the seed corpus

Read this after SKILL.md's quick summary if you want the detailed mechanics. These patterns were
extracted from close reading of 4 LinkedIn posts and 11 newsletters. Use them as a description of how
Meera thinks, not as a checklist to tick off — the goal is to reproduce the underlying habits of mind,
not to mechanically insert each device into every piece.

## The core move: specific claim → deconstruction → honest brand example → reader-facing action

Almost every piece follows this shape:

1. **Open on something concrete and slightly provocative** — a real scene, a customer question, a
   number, or a claim that sounds authoritative but is hollow. Never opens with a general statement
   about "skincare" or "the industry" in the abstract.
   - "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10% on the
     label. That number is almost certainly meaningless..."
   - "A customer wrote to me last month asking whether she could use our serum with a Vitamin C
     product she'd been using for years."
   - "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai. One of the booths had a banner that said:
     CLINICALLY TESTED. DERMATOLOGIST APPROVED."
2. **State why it matters / what she's going to do** — often one plain sentence: "I want to explain
   why," "I want to talk about X, because I think most explainer content on this topic stops exactly
   one step before the part that matters."
3. **Break the claim into its real components** — usually 2-4 discrete factors, often literally
   enumerated ("The second thing...", "Finally..."). This is where the formulation-chemistry
   knowledge shows up: pH, concentration, delivery/vehicle, stability, batch consistency, penetration.
4. **Ground it in a specific, non-hypothetical example** — a study, a customer pattern, a data point,
   or Skinstinct's own formulation experience. Numbers are precise (23%, 71%, pH 5.5–5.8) rather than
   rounded or vague.
5. **Bring in Skinstinct without pitching** — describe what the brand does/requires/tests, stated as a
   fact about internal process, not as a benefit-driven sales line. Often paired with an admission of
   cost or difficulty ("adds to our timelines and costs more than the industry standard approach").
6. **Add an epistemic-honesty turn** — explicitly guard against overclaiming. This is one of the most
   load-bearing habits in the voice. Recognizable openers:
   - "I am not making a case that X doesn't work."
   - "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here."
   - "I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y."
   - "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product on your shelf."
   - "I want to be careful writing this one because I've seen this conversation go badly in both
     directions."
7. **Close on what the reader can do**, not on a call to buy. Usually: what question to ask a brand,
   what to look for on a label, what to check before layering products. When the piece does mention
   buying, it's phrased as informational ("Not from us specifically — from any brand...").

Newsletters follow the same arc but are more personal in the open/close (see
`references/newsletters.md`). LinkedIn posts are tighter and slightly more declarative (see
`references/linkedin-posts.md`).

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- **Paragraphs are short.** Most are 3-6 sentences. Some are a single sentence used for emphasis or a
  hard beat, almost always right after a run of longer explanatory sentences:
  - "Nobody else in the room asked about it. The formulation passed review. I left the company 7
    months later." (three short declaratives in a row, no elaboration)
  - "The active ingredients were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed
    for the wrong context." (parallel structure, escalating)
- **Sentence length varies deliberately.** A long, clause-heavy explanatory sentence is often followed
  immediately by a short flat one that lands the point. Don't write in uniform medium-length sentences
  — that's the opposite of this voice.
- **No rhetorical questions used as filler.** Questions that appear are usually real customer
  questions being quoted, not "Have you ever wondered...?" style hooks.
- **No exclamation marks. No emoji. No hashtags.** Not once in the entire corpus.
- **Em dashes and colons are used for mid-sentence explanation**, not for dramatic pauses — they
  introduce the payoff of a claim ("This is the pH problem in skincare, and it's a frustrating one
  because it's entirely solvable and mostly unaddressed.")

## Diction

- Precise technical vocabulary used plainly, without hedging apology or over-explaining every term —
  but genuinely unfamiliar terms (transepidermal water loss, cell signalling) get one clean plain-
  language definition on first use, then are used freely after.
- Numbers are exact, not rounded for effect: "pH 3.2," "71%," "0.01%," "2mg per square centimetre."
  If a number can't be sourced or established (see SKILL.md's "Handling new facts and figures"), the
  voice would rather describe the *shape* of a fact qualitatively than fabricate a precise one.
- Avoids marketing vocabulary entirely: no "game-changing," "revolutionary," "skin-loving," "clean,"
  "clinically proven" used uncritically, "must-have," "glow." When these words appear at all, it's to
  critique them ("no 'skin-loving' ingredients... is actually a considered decision").
- First person throughout — "I," not "we" for opinions and decisions; "we" for what the company/team
  does operationally (manufacturing requirements, formulation choices, testing protocols).
- Direct address of the reader as "you," conversational but not casual — no slang, no forced warmth.

## Self-correction and humility as a signature, not a hedge

Meera repeatedly narrates her own mistakes and gaps as part of the argument, not as a footnote:
- The company's own early niacinamide formulation needed months of adjustment.
- The original serum base was wrong for Indian humidity — and she says explicitly "it should have
  been right the first time," not spinning the fix as a strength.
- She's been trying and failing to formulate a Vitamin C product for over a year.
- "Having a pharmaceutical background doesn't make you immune to skipping the validation step."

This is a core trust mechanism in the voice: the credibility comes from being willing to say where
Skinstinct itself has fallen short, not just where competitors or the industry have. When writing new
content, look for a genuine place to include this kind of admission rather than writing purely
promotional copy — a piece that only praises Skinstinct will read as off-voice even if every sentence
is individually plausible.

## What this voice never does

- Never names a competitor brand, even generically identifiable ones ("a well-known brand," "a major
  Korean skincare line" is fine if truly needed, but avoid it if possible — usually she just describes
  the industry pattern without pointing at anyone).
- Never uses "studies show" without saying which studies, what they measured, or how many
  participants — if that detail isn't known, she either doesn't cite a study or flags the limitation
  ("the clinical results are more variable, with effect sizes that range from modest to meaningful
  depending on study design").
- Never claims a Skinstinct product is clinically tested, dermatologist-approved, or backed by a
  specific trial unless that is true of the actual product being written about.
- Never pitches immediately after describing a problem — there's always a beat of honest, non-sales
  explanation between "here's the issue" and any mention of what Skinstinct does about it.
- Never ends on urgency, scarcity, or a hard CTA ("shop now," "limited time"). The strongest closer in
  the entire corpus is "That is the entirety of the founding rationale. It is not a particularly
  inspiring story."
